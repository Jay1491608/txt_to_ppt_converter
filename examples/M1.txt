## TOPIC 1.1 — What Is Artificial Intelligence?

### Slide 1 — Section Slide
**Title:** What Is Artificial Intelligence?

---

### Slide 2 — Defining AI, Machine Learning, and Automation
**Bullets:**
- **Artificial Intelligence (AI)** — Technology that enables computers to perform tasks that normally require human thinking, such as recognising patterns or making decisions.
- **Machine Learning (ML)** — A subset of AI where systems learn from data and improve over time, rather than following fixed instructions.
- **Automation** — Software that follows pre-set rules to complete repetitive tasks; it does not "learn" or adapt.
- **The key difference** — Automation follows a fixed script; AI and ML can adjust their output based on new information.
- **Why this matters for government** — Knowing these differences helps departments choose the right tool for the right problem, rather than treating all "smart technology" as the same thing.
- **A simple analogy** — Automation is like a vending machine; AI is more like an assistant who learns your preferences over time.

**Speaker Notes:** Open by acknowledging that these terms are often used interchangeably in the media, which causes confusion in policy discussions. Emphasise that clarity here will support better decision-making later in the course, particularly when officials are asked to approve budgets or projects labelled "AI." Use the vending machine versus assistant analogy to anchor the distinction in something relatable. Reassure the audience that they do not need a technical background to grasp these ideas — only a clear mental model.

**Q&A:**
1. **Q:** Is all automation considered AI? **A:** No. Automation follows fixed rules; AI systems can learn and adapt based on data.
2. **Q:** What is the simplest way to describe machine learning? **A:** It's a method where a computer improves its performance by learning from examples rather than being explicitly programmed for every scenario.
3. **Q:** Why does this distinction matter for procurement decisions? **A:** Vendors sometimes market basic automation as "AI"; understanding the difference helps avoid overpaying for simpler tools.
4. **Q:** Can a system be both automated and use AI? **A:** Yes, many modern systems combine rule-based automation with AI components for different parts of a process.
5. **Q:** Does AI always involve learning from data? **A:** Most modern AI, especially machine learning, does rely on data, though some early AI systems used fixed logical rules instead.

---

### Slide 3 — How AI Differs from Traditional Software
**Bullets:**
- **Traditional software** — Follows instructions written by a programmer; the outcome is predictable and repeatable every time.
- **AI-based software** — Learns patterns from data and can produce different outputs as it encounters new situations.
- **Rule-based vs. pattern-based** — Traditional software says "if X, then Y"; AI says "based on what I've seen, this is likely."
- **Adaptability** — AI systems can improve their accuracy over time with more data and feedback.
- **Uncertainty is normal** — Unlike traditional software, AI outputs often come with a level of probability rather than a guaranteed correct answer.
- **Implication for oversight** — Because AI is probabilistic, human review remains important, especially for consequential decisions.

**Speaker Notes:** This slide helps participants understand why AI systems require different governance approaches than traditional IT systems. Stress that "probabilistic" does not mean "unreliable" — it means outputs should be understood as informed estimates that may still need human judgement, particularly in sensitive government contexts like benefits eligibility or law enforcement support tools.

**Q&A:**
1. **Q:** Why can't AI guarantee the same answer every time? **A:** Because it makes probability-based predictions from patterns in data, rather than following a fixed rule for every case.
2. **Q:** Does this mean AI is less trustworthy than traditional software? **A:** Not necessarily — it means AI requires different testing, monitoring, and human oversight practices.
3. **Q:** What kind of government decisions need extra human review when AI is involved? **A:** High-impact decisions such as those affecting eligibility, rights, or safety typically require human review.
4. **Q:** Can traditional software also make mistakes? **A:** Yes, but its mistakes usually come from bugs in the code, not from misjudging patterns in data.
5. **Q:** Is it possible to combine traditional software reliability with AI's adaptability? **A:** Yes, many systems use AI for prediction and traditional rules for final checks or safeguards.

### Slide 4 — The Building Blocks of AI Systems
**Bullets:**
- **Data** — The raw information (numbers, text, images) that AI systems learn from; quality data leads to better outcomes.
- **Algorithms** — The mathematical instructions that process data and identify patterns.
- **Models** — The result of training an algorithm on data; a model is what actually makes predictions or generates content.
- **Training** — The process of feeding data into an algorithm so it can learn and build a model.
- **Infrastructure** — The computing power (servers, cloud platforms) needed to run AI systems at scale.
- **Feedback loops** — Ongoing input that helps refine a model's accuracy after deployment.

**Speaker Notes:** Use a simple cooking analogy: data is the ingredients, the algorithm is the recipe method, and the model is the finished dish that gets served repeatedly. Highlight that "garbage in, garbage out" applies strongly to AI — poor-quality government data (incomplete records, biased historical data) will produce poor-quality AI outcomes, regardless of how advanced the algorithm is.

**Q&A:**
1. **Q:** What's the difference between an algorithm and a model? **A:** An algorithm is the method used to learn; a model is the trained result that makes actual predictions.
2. **Q:** Why is data quality so important in government AI projects? **A:** Because AI learns from data patterns, poor or biased data will lead to poor or biased outcomes.
3. **Q:** What does "training" mean in the AI context? **A:** It refers to the process of running data through an algorithm so it can learn patterns and produce a usable model.
4. **Q:** Do government departments need their own servers to use AI? **A:** Not necessarily — many use cloud-based platforms rather than building their own infrastructure.
5. **Q:** Why do AI models need ongoing feedback after launch? **A:** Because real-world conditions change, and feedback helps keep the model accurate and relevant over time.

- **Predictive maintenance** — Anticipating equipment failures in public infrastructure like transport or utilities.
- **Fraud detection** — Flagging unusual patterns in benefit claims or tax filings for review.
- **Translation and accessibility tools** — Helping government communications reach citizens in multiple languages.
- **Data-driven planning** — Supporting urban planning and resource allocation with pattern analysis.

**Speaker Notes:** Ground the theoretical concepts from earlier slides in relatable, low-risk examples that participants likely already encounter. Note that these are illustrative examples of AI applications, not endorsements of specific vendors or products. Encourage participants to think of one example from their own department where a similar pattern might apply, setting up discussion for later modules.

**Q&A:**

2. **Q:** How does AI help with document processing? **A:** It can read and extract key information from forms, reducing manual data entry.
3. **Q:** Can AI fully replace human review in fraud detection? **A:** No, AI typically flags unusual patterns for human investigators to review further.
4. **Q:** Why is predictive maintenance useful for public infrastructure? **A:** It helps identify potential equipment failures before they happen, reducing costly disruptions.
5. **Q:** Does AI improve accessibility for citizens? **A:** Yes, tools like translation and text-to-speech can make government services more accessible to diverse populations.

## TOPIC 1.2 — Types of AI

### Slide 6 — Section Slide
**Title:** Types of AI

---

### Slide 7 — Narrow AI vs. General AI
**Bullets:**
- **Narrow AI (Weak AI)** — Designed to perform one specific task well, such as translating text or detecting spam.
- **General AI (Strong AI)** — A theoretical form of AI that could perform any intellectual task a human can; this does not yet exist.
- **All current AI is narrow** — Every AI system in use today, including advanced tools, falls into the narrow AI category.
- **Task-specific strength** — Narrow AI can outperform humans in its specific task but cannot transfer that skill to unrelated tasks.
- **Why the distinction matters** — Understanding this prevents unrealistic expectations about what today's AI tools can actually do.
- **Government relevance** — Public sector AI projects should be scoped around specific, well-defined tasks rather than open-ended goals.

**Speaker Notes:** This is a foundational slide for managing expectations throughout the rest of the course. Some participants may have seen science-fiction depictions of general AI and assume today's tools are close to that level. Clarify firmly but neutrally that this is not the case, and that all deployed government AI tools are narrow, task-specific systems.

**Q&A:**
1. **Q:** Does general AI exist today? **A:** No, general AI remains a theoretical concept; all currently deployed AI systems are narrow AI.
2. **Q:** Can a narrow AI system designed for translation also handle scheduling tasks? **A:** Not without being specifically built or retrained for that purpose.
3. **Q:** Why should government AI projects be narrowly scoped? **A:** Narrow, well-defined tasks are easier to test, govern, and hold accountable for accurate results.
4. **Q:** Is narrow AI less useful because it's limited to one task? **A:** No, it can still deliver significant value by performing that task more efficiently than manual methods.
5. **Q:** How does understanding this distinction help with public communication? **A:** It helps set realistic expectations with citizens and stakeholders about what a given AI tool can and cannot do.

---

### Slide 8 — Machine Learning Explained
**Bullets:**
- **Supervised learning** — The system learns from labelled examples, such as past applications marked "approved" or "rejected."
- **Unsupervised learning** — The system finds patterns in data without labelled examples, useful for grouping similar cases.
- **Reinforcement learning** — The system learns through trial and error, receiving rewards for good outcomes.
- **Training data requirement** — All machine learning approaches require sufficient, representative data to perform well.
- **Common government use** — Supervised learning is the most commonly used approach for tasks like classification and prediction.
- **Limitation** — ML systems can only learn from patterns present in their training data, not from situations they've never encountered.

**Speaker Notes:** Keep this slide conceptual rather than mathematical. Use a simple example: a supervised learning system for grant applications is trained on thousands of past applications labelled as "funded" or "not funded" to learn what characteristics correlate with funding decisions. Note that this creates a responsibility to ensure historical data used for training does not carry forward past inequities.

**Q&A:**
1. **Q:** What's the difference between supervised and unsupervised learning? **A:** Supervised learning uses labelled examples to learn, while unsupervised learning finds patterns without labels.
2. **Q:** Which type of machine learning is most common in government use cases? **A:** Supervised learning is generally the most widely used for classification and prediction tasks.
3. **Q:** What is reinforcement learning best suited for? **A:** Tasks involving sequential decision-making, where a system learns through trial, error, and reward.
4. **Q:** Why is training data quality a governance concern? **A:** If historical data reflects past biases, the trained system may reproduce those biases in its outputs.
5. **Q:** Can machine learning handle situations it has never seen before? **A:** It struggles with situations very different from its training data, which is why ongoing monitoring is important.

---

### Slide 9 — Deep Learning and Neural Networks
**Bullets:**
- **Neural networks** — Computing systems loosely inspired by the human brain, made up of interconnected layers that process information.
- **Deep learning** — Machine learning using neural networks with many layers, capable of handling complex patterns like images and language.
- **Why "deep"** — The term refers to the number of processing layers, not necessarily the complexity of the task.
- **Strength** — Deep learning excels at tasks like image recognition, speech processing, and language understanding.
- **Resource intensity** — Deep learning models typically require large amounts of data and computing power to train.
- **Foundation for generative AI** — Most modern generative AI tools, including chatbots, are built using deep learning techniques.

**Speaker Notes:** Keep the technical depth light here; the goal is familiarity with the term "deep learning" rather than technical mastery. Mention that this is the technology underpinning tools like the ones discussed in Topic 1.6, so participants can connect the dots between today's terminology and tomorrow's practical tools.

**Q&A:**
1. **Q:** What does "deep" refer to in deep learning? **A:** It refers to the number of layers in the neural network used to process data, not the complexity of the task itself.
2. **Q:** What tasks is deep learning particularly good at? **A:** Tasks like image recognition, speech processing, and natural language understanding.
3. **Q:** Does deep learning require more resources than simpler machine learning methods? **A:** Yes, it typically requires larger datasets and significantly more computing power.
4. **Q:** Is deep learning the same as generative AI? **A:** No, deep learning is a technique; generative AI is a category of applications often built using deep learning.
5. **Q:** Why is understanding neural networks useful for a non-technical government official? **A:** It helps demystify the technology behind AI tools and supports more informed conversations with technical teams.

---

### Slide 10 — Generative AI: What Makes It Different
**Bullets:**
- **Definition** — AI that creates new content, such as text, images, or code, rather than only analysing or classifying existing data.
- **Contrast with predictive AI** — Predictive AI estimates outcomes (like risk scores); generative AI produces new material.
- **How it works, simply put** — It learns patterns from vast amounts of existing content and uses those patterns to generate new, similar content.
- **Common outputs** — Written reports, summaries, translated text, images, and even basic code.
- **Government applications** — Drafting communications, summarising documents, and supporting research tasks.
- **Important caveat** — Generative AI can produce plausible-sounding but incorrect information, so verification remains essential.

**Speaker Notes:** This slide serves as a bridge to Topic 1.6, where LLMs are covered in more depth. Emphasise the caveat about accuracy strongly here, as it is one of the most important practical takeaways of the entire module — generative AI outputs should always be reviewed by a knowledgeable person before being used in official government contexts.

**Q&A:**
1. **Q:** How is generative AI different from predictive AI? **A:** Predictive AI estimates outcomes or classifications, while generative AI creates new content such as text or images.
2. **Q:** What kinds of content can generative AI produce? **A:** Written text, summaries, translations, images, and basic code, among other formats.
3. **Q:** Why should generative AI outputs be reviewed before official use? **A:** Because these tools can produce inaccurate or fabricated information that sounds plausible but isn't correct.
4. **Q:** What government tasks could benefit from generative AI? **A:** Drafting routine communications, summarising lengthy documents, and supporting initial research.
5. **Q:** Does generative AI understand what it's writing the way a person does? **A:** No, it generates content based on learned patterns rather than genuine understanding or reasoning.

### Slide 22 — Core Terms: Algorithm, Model, Data
**Bullets:**
- **Algorithm** — A defined set of mathematical steps used to process data and identify patterns.
- **Model** — The trained output of an algorithm, used to make predictions or generate content on new data.
- **Dataset** — A structured collection of data used to train, test, or validate an AI system.
- **Training data** — The specific portion of data used to teach a model to recognise patterns.
- **Test data** — A separate portion of data used to evaluate how well a model performs on new, unseen examples.
- **Parameters** — Internal values a model adjusts during training to improve its accuracy.

**Speaker Notes:** This glossary slide is meant as a quick-reference tool participants can return to throughout the course. Encourage participants to bookmark or note these terms, as they will recur in later modules covering specific use cases and governance frameworks.

**Q&A:**
1. **Q:** What's the difference between training data and test data? **A:** Training data teaches the model to recognise patterns, while test data evaluates how well it performs on new, unseen examples.
2. **Q:** What are parameters in an AI model? **A:** Internal values the model adjusts during training to improve its accuracy over time.
3. **Q:** Is a dataset the same as a database? **A:** Not exactly — a dataset is a specific, structured collection of data used for AI purposes, while a database is a broader storage system.
4. **Q:** Why is it important to separate training and test data? **A:** It helps confirm the model can perform well on new cases, not just the examples it was trained on.
5. **Q:** Can a single algorithm produce different models? **A:** Yes, the same algorithm can produce different models depending on the specific data it's trained on.

---

### Slide 23 — Training and Learning Terms
**Bullets:**
- **Training** — The process of teaching a model to recognise patterns using labelled or unlabelled data.
- **Fine-tuning** — Further training an existing model on a smaller, specific dataset to improve performance for a particular task.
- **Overfitting** — When a model learns training data too precisely and performs poorly on new, unseen data.
- **Bias (in AI)** — Systematic errors in a model's outputs, often stemming from imbalances in training data.
- **Accuracy** — A measure of how often a model's predictions or outputs are correct.
- **Validation** — The process of checking a model's performance during training to catch issues before deployment.

**Speaker Notes:** Spend extra time on "bias" and "overfitting," as these terms will be directly relevant to later governance and ethics discussions in the course. Keep explanations concrete: for example, overfitting is like memorising exam answers rather than understanding the underlying subject.

### Slide 27 — What Is a Large Language Model?
**Bullets:**
- **Definition** — A Large Language Model (LLM) is an AI system trained on vast amounts of text to understand and generate human-like language.
- **Scale matters** — LLMs are trained on enormous datasets, often including books, articles, and websites.
- **Pattern-based understanding** — LLMs predict likely word sequences based on patterns learned during training, rather than truly "understanding" meaning as humans do.
- **General-purpose capability** — Unlike narrow, single-task AI tools, LLMs can assist with a wide range of language-based tasks.
- **Examples of use** — Drafting text, summarising documents, answering questions, and translating language.
- **Not a search engine** — LLMs generate responses based on learned patterns, not by retrieving information from a live, verified database, unless specifically connected to one.

**Speaker Notes:** This is a critical conceptual slide. Make clear that LLMs do not "know" facts the way a database does — they generate the most statistically likely response based on patterns, which explains both their usefulness and their limitations. This sets up the accuracy caveat that will recur throughout the rest of the topic.

**Q&A:**
1. **Q:** Does an LLM "understand" language the way a person does? **A:** Not in the human sense — it predicts likely word patterns based on its training data, rather than genuinely comprehending meaning.
2. **Q:** Is an LLM the same as a search engine? **A:** No, unless specifically connected to search tools, an LLM generates responses from learned patterns rather than retrieving live, verified information.
3. **Q:** What kinds of tasks can LLMs help with? **A:** Drafting text, summarising documents, answering questions, and translating language, among other language-based tasks.
4. **Q:** What data are LLMs typically trained on? **A:** Large collections of text such as books, articles, and websites.
5. **Q:** Why are LLMs described as "general-purpose" compared to narrow AI tools? **A:** Because they can assist with a wide variety of language tasks rather than being built for just one specific function.

### Slide 28 — How LLMs Generate Responses
**Bullets:**
- **Tokenisation** — Text is broken down into small units called tokens before processing.
- **Pattern prediction** — The model predicts the most likely next token based on the input and everything it has learned.
- **Sequential generation** — Responses are built one token at a time, with each new token informed by what came before.
- **No internal fact-checking** — LLMs do not automatically verify the accuracy of their own outputs unless specific tools are added.
- **Influence of the prompt** — The quality and clarity of the input prompt significantly affects the quality of the generated response.
- **Consistency varies** — The same prompt can sometimes produce slightly different responses, reflecting the model's probabilistic nature.

**Speaker Notes:** Avoid going too deep into technical mechanics; the goal is functional understanding, not engineering detail. The key takeaway for a government audience is that response quality depends heavily on prompt clarity, and that verification of outputs remains a human responsibility.

**Q&A:**
1. **Q:** What is "tokenisation" in simple terms? **A:** The process of breaking text into small units, called tokens, that the model processes to generate a response.
2. **Q:** Does the model check its own answers for accuracy? **A:** Not automatically — verification is generally the responsibility of the user unless additional fact-checking tools are integrated.
3. **Q:** Why might the same prompt produce different answers at different times? **A:** Because the model generates responses probabilistically, which can lead to some variation between attempts.
4. **Q:** How does prompt quality affect the output? **A:** Clearer, more specific prompts tend to produce more accurate and useful responses.
5. **Q:** Is the response generated all at once or built gradually? **A:** It's generated sequentially, one token at a time, with each part informed by what came before it.
